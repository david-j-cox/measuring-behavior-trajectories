Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Testing Behavior Dynamic Models to Predict Choice in a Non-Stationary Two-Choice Foraging Environment," for publication in the *Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior*.

In the 1992 JEAB special issue on behavior dynamics, several authors argued that the temporal structure of behavior — transient adjustments, oscillations, path dependence, and sensitivity to perturbation — constituted a legitimate scientific subject matter for behavior analysts, and that dynamic process models would be needed to account for it. A number of competing accounts were proposed during the 1970s–1990s (e.g., melioration, the kinetic model, hill-climbing, behavioral momentum), but these models were rarely fit to the same dataset, rarely compared against alternatives from adjacent fields, and rarely evaluated on response-by-response trajectories under explicitly non-stationary conditions. The present manuscript revisits that program of work directly.

We collected trial-level choice data from 60 humans completing a four-phase, two-option foraging task that included regime shifts, contingency reversals, and perturbation probes. Twenty-two models were fit to the same data and compared on the same footing: three baseline heuristics, six historical behavior-analytic models, three reinforcement-learning variants, three hidden Markov models, three foraging models, and four empirical dynamical models (EDM). Models were evaluated using AIC and next-response predictive accuracy. State-switching architectures (HMMs) provided the best account of the data, followed by EDM and reinforcement-learning models, with the historical behavior-analytic models in the middle of the pack and baselines clearly worst. The pattern suggests that accounts allowing latent regime changes capture features of choice in non-stationary environments that smoother, single-process models do not.

We believe the manuscript fits JEAB for three reasons. First, its central question — how does choice adjust over time when contingencies change? — is the question that motivated the 1992 special issue, and progress on it has been uneven in the decades since. Second, the manuscript puts behavior-analytic models in direct, quantitative competition with models from reinforcement learning, dynamical systems, and foraging theory on the same dataset, which we hope is useful to readers regardless of which tradition they work in. Third, all models, code, and data are organized for reuse so that other investigators can extend the comparison or apply the same evaluation framework to their own datasets.

The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and has not been previously published. There are no conflicts of interest to disclose, and the work received no external funding. AI-assisted tooling (Claude Code) was used during code development and analysis, as disclosed on the title page; the author reviewed all code and takes full responsibility for the content.
